The part nobody has time for

Work arrives faster than anyone can sort it.

Every message, ticket, form and row that lands in your stack carries one small decision: what is this, who needs it, what happens next. On their own they take a minute. Together they take the day.

A support inbox where the urgent one sits three screens down.
A CRM where the call notes get written tomorrow, if at all.
A form that waits until someone turns it into a ticket by hand.

None of it needs a whole person. All of it needs judgment.

Autonomy is not a setting we chose for you. It is the sentence you wrote, and you can rewrite it in a minute.

1Listen

Work arrives.

An event fires in a connected app, a schedule comes due, or you press go. The agent wakes on the trigger you chose, so it starts when the work shows up, checked on a schedule you can see.

2Understand

The AI reads it.

It classifies what arrived, pulls out the fields that matter, and weighs it against everything else waiting. This is the step a rules engine cannot do, and the reason a real model sits in the middle of the flow.

3Act

The next step happens.

A reply drafted, a ticket filed, a row updated, a document written. The agent performs real operations in your connected apps, using your own access, so the result shows up where your team already looks.

4Decide

You set how far it goes.

Ask for drafts and everything waits for your review. Tell it how far to go, and it stops there. A draft waits for your click. You can split the line down the middle by rule, and you can move it whenever you like.

Adaptation

It fits your process. Not the other way around.

Most automation asks you to redraw your work as a diagram before it will help. An agent asks you to describe it. That difference is why it survives contact with how your team actually operates.

It works where the work already is

There is no new inbox to check and no dashboard to remember. The agent reads and writes inside Gmail, Slack, Jira, Sheets and the rest of your stack, so its output lands in the place your team was going to open anyway.

What this changesAdoption stops being a training problem. Nobody has to learn a new tool to receive the benefit.

Written, not wired

You brief an agent the way you would brief a new teammate: what to watch, what matters, what to do about it, and what to leave alone. Plain sentences, in your own words. To change a rule, you edit the sentence.

What this changesThe person who knows the process can change the process, without waiting for whoever built the flowchart.
